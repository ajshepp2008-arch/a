# Chapter 1: The Event

---

There is a version of my father I remember from before. He was a steady man. Not unfeeling — steady. The kind of person who, when the house got loud, became quieter, as if he understood that someone had to be the fixed point everyone else could orient around. He kept his tools organized. He remembered the names of people he'd met once, years ago. He held a consistent idea of who he was and where he stood.

Then something happened to him.

I am not going to tell you exactly what it was. That is his story, and it belongs to him, and the details are not the point. The point is the category of the event: it was the kind of thing that activates every alarm the body has. It was acute, it was terrifying, and for a sustained period of time, he genuinely did not know if he was going to survive it — not just physically, but as a person with a coherent inner life.

He survived. And then something else began.

---

## The First Sign

It started small. A wrench he was certain he'd left on the workbench in the garage was not there. He looked for it. He searched thoroughly, the way he searched for everything — methodically, patiently. It was not there. He bought a replacement.

A week later, the original was on the workbench. Exactly where he'd left it.

He mentioned it to my mother. She didn't know what he was talking about. She hadn't moved it. As far as she knew, there had only ever been one wrench, and it had always been there.

He let it go.

But things kept not being where they should be. And conversations kept not having happened. He would reference something he and a friend had discussed at length — plans they'd made, things they'd agreed on — and the friend would have no memory of it. Not "I forgot" — *no memory*, as if it had never occurred.

He started writing things down. Dates, conversations, locations of objects. He needed to confirm that his memory was real. That the world he was living in matched the world he'd experienced.

It didn't.

Not always. Not consistently. But enough that he couldn't explain it away.

---

## The Theory

It took him a long time to arrive at the idea he eventually landed on. He's not a mystical person. He doesn't reach for supernatural explanations. He arrived at this theory the way he arrived at most things — by sitting with a problem until the evidence pointed somewhere.

What he came to believe was this:

When the event happened — the thing that shook him to his foundation — he had not simply *changed*. He had shifted. Like a record skipping to a groove that was almost identical to the one before, but not quite. He was in a reality that was nearly the same as the one he'd known. Same house. Same family. Same city. Same life, in almost every particular.

But not the same wrench. Not the same conversation with his friend. Not the same arrangement of small things.

He had transferred. And the place he'd transferred into was close — so close that no one around him could see the difference. They were still in the same place they'd always been. He was the only one who had moved.

---

## What This Means

I want to be careful here, at the outset, about what I am and am not claiming on my father's behalf.

I am not claiming that this is literally, physically, scientifically what happened. I am not writing a book about multiverse theory or quantum mechanics.

I am claiming that this is what he experienced. I am claiming that his experience deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. And I am claiming that his framework — the idea of *fracturing points*, of transfer into near-identical realities — is a profound and useful way of describing something that trauma does to people, something that existing clinical language does not fully capture.

When he says "I transferred," he is describing an experience that is real. The wrench was not there. The conversations did not happen. The world had been quietly, wrongly rearranged. This is not psychosis. It is not fabrication. It is something that happens to people who have been through the kind of event that rewrites the fundamental parameters of existence.

This book is an attempt to understand it.

---

## Before We Go Further

If you have been through something like what my father went through, you may recognize what I'm describing. You may have had the experience of returning to a world that is technically the same as the one you left but feels wrong in ways you cannot name. You may have lost trust in your own memory, or in the reliability of the external world, or in both.

If that is where you are, I want you to know something before we go any further:

You are not broken. You are not lying. You are not losing your mind.

You are standing at a fracturing point. And this book is about what that means.
