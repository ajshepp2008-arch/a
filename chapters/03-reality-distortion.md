# Chapter 3: Reality Distortion

---

## The World After the Fracture

After a fracture, the world does not announce that it has changed. It continues to present itself as reliable, consistent, and shared. The sun rises on schedule. People go about their lives. Traffic lights change in the expected order.

But for the person who has fractured, there is a persistent, low-level *wrongness* that underlies all of this normal surface. It is not a wrongness that can be pointed to. It cannot be photographed or measured or confirmed by others. It is a wrongness in the texture of things — in the feeling of moving through a world that fits almost perfectly but not quite.

This chapter is about what that wrongness looks, feels, and sounds like in lived experience. And why it is not what it is usually assumed to be.

---

## The Problem of Missing Objects

The missing object is often the first visible sign of fracture — and the one most easily dismissed.

An object is not where it should be. The person who fractured knows where they left it. They are certain. It was there. Now it is not there. Later it may reappear, in the original location, as if it never left.

From the outside, this is easily explained: the person misremembered, or someone else moved it, or they simply weren't paying attention when they put it down. These explanations are reasonable. They are also often wrong.

What distinguishes fracture-related displacement from ordinary forgetfulness is persistence and pattern. It is not one object, one time. It is multiple objects, repeatedly, in ways that follow no rational logic of forgetfulness. And it is accompanied by a quality of *certainty* — not the fuzzy confidence of someone who thinks they remember, but the clear, specific certainty of someone who actually does.

The person who has fractured does not misremember. They remember correctly. The wrench was there because, in the reality they came from, the wrench was there. In the reality they transferred into, it was not.

This is not a comfortable explanation. It is a true one.

---

## The Problem of Unshared Conversations

The unshared conversation is more disturbing than the missing object, because it involves other people.

A person who has fractured may have a clear, detailed memory of a significant conversation with someone close to them — a discussion, an agreement, a moment of genuine connection. They remember what was said. They remember the emotional quality of it. They remember where they were sitting, what the light was like, what was on the table.

The other person has no memory of it.

Not a fuzzy memory. Not an uncertain one. No memory. As if it did not happen.

From the outside, this is again easily explained: one of them is wrong. Either the fractured person fabricated the memory (consciously or unconsciously) or the other person has forgotten. One of these things must be true, because two people cannot have been in the same conversation only one of them remembers.

But here is the thing about fracture: they were not, in a precise sense, in the same place. The conversation happened. It happened in the reality the fractured person came from. The person they spoke with — inhabiting the destination reality all along — was not there for it.

This creates a particular kind of loneliness. To remember something that no one else confirms is to have your reality treated as an error. To know that you are not wrong, but to have no way to prove it.

---

## Spatial Wrongness

The third major manifestation of reality distortion is harder to describe because it is less concrete.

Familiar places feel wrong. Not dramatically wrong — not transformed, not alien. Subtly, persistently, uncategorisably wrong. The kitchen is in the right place. The furniture is where it should be. And yet.

The feeling is something like the feeling of walking into a stage set. Everything is in its correct position, but the sense that these objects constitute a *real* place — that they have weight and history and continuity — is diminished. The room looks like the room but does not feel like the room.

Some people describe this as a loss of warmth. Places that used to feel inhabited — marked by their own history, their own smell, their own particular light — feel depopulated of meaning. They are correct in all their details but emptied of whatever it was that made them *theirs*.

This is not a psychological metaphor for grief. It is a perceptual reality. The place is subtly different from the place the person came from, and that difference — small enough to be invisible but large enough to be felt — registers as wrongness.

---

## The Accumulation Effect

Here is where fracture becomes compounding.

A single fracture produces a reality that is very close to the original. The seams are narrow. The distortions are minor. The displaced objects and unshared conversations are manageable.

But fractures accumulate.

Each significant traumatic event — each moment of sufficient force — produces another transfer. And each transfer carries its own drift. The new reality is close to the previous one, but the previous one was already close to the one before it, which was already close to the original.

Over time, the distance compounds. A person who has fractured many times may inhabit a reality that is, at the surface level, entirely recognizable — but that has drifted so far from their original starting point that the accumulated differences have become immense.

For such a person, the distortion is no longer occasional and specific. It is pervasive. The wrongness is everywhere. The map and the terrain are so far out of alignment that navigation becomes genuinely difficult.

Not impossible. But genuinely difficult.

---

## What Distortion Is Not

It is important to distinguish reality distortion, as described here, from the clinical categories it superficially resembles.

**Derealization** — the clinical experience in which the external world seems unreal, dreamlike, or distant — shares some features with reality distortion. But derealization is typically understood as a perception problem, a failure of the self to feel fully present in an otherwise unchanged world. What I am describing is different: the world has changed. The self is present. The problem is not presence but accuracy.

**Depersonalization** — the experience of feeling detached from one's own thoughts, feelings, and body — is closer in some ways to identity dissolution, which is the subject of the next chapter. But again, it is understood as a perception problem rather than an accurate perception of a changed reality.

**Psychosis** — the most common misreading of fracture experiences — involves a break from shared reality, typically characterized by beliefs and perceptions that have no basis in any accessible external reality. Fracture is different: the experiences have a basis. The wrench was there. The conversation happened. The place has changed. The fractured person is not inventing things. They are reporting with perfect accuracy on a reality that others cannot access.

This distinction matters. It matters because it changes the response. A person who is psychotic needs to be brought back into contact with shared reality. A person who has fractured needs something different: they need their experience to be respected, their reality to be believed, and help rebuilding a stable ground beneath a changed world.

Those are not the same things. Treating them as the same is one of the cruelest mistakes we make.
